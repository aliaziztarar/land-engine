# What the user receives after a site review

Write in plain British English for someone who may be new to planning. Explain each term the first time. Every finding carries its source (document and page, or web address) and the date it was read. Do not leave anything out because it "goes without saying". Do not repeat the SearchLand panel back as findings: the user has seen it. Mention a SearchLand fact only where you add something to it (what it means, whether it is right, what lies behind it).

```
# <Site name>, <TITLE>

**In one line:** <the verdict in plain words, e.g. "Worth writing to the owners: the council is short of housing land and has approved similar village edge schemes nearby.">

## 1. The land
In two or three lines only (the user has the SearchLand panel): what it is and who owns it. Then what matters beyond the panel: what each register entry means for us, who else is involved, and anything the panel gets wrong or leaves out.

## 2. What the council has said about this land
Each assessment round and its verdict, the key sentences quoted, any allocation, the call for sites.

## 3. Where the council stands
Not the figures (the panel shows them) but what they mean for this site: whether the tilted balance applies and how much weight it carried in recent decisions, where the plan is heading, and whether the settlement's tier and boundary help or hurt.

## 4. What a scheme here would have to provide
Affordable housing percentage and tenure, mix and space rules, density, other obligations, and a first range of homes with its assumptions.

## 5. Planning history of the site and its neighbours
Each application and appeal that matters, why it was decided as it was, and then the reasons tested one by one:

| Reason (as worded) | Today | Why, with evidence | How it could be resolved |
|---|---|---|---|

## 5a. What earlier sites in this council tell us
Where the council memory changes the reading of this site (same policy, same gap, same officer reasoning, same inspector outcome), with the earlier title. Omit if this is the first site in the council.

## 6. How this council behaves (precedent)
The two examples (one approved, one refused, most recent first, within 10 years): reference, homes, decision, and the reasons that mattered, then what they suggest: what gets approved, what gets refused and why, how appeals go, and what that means for this site. State that this rests on two examples.

## 7. What the local plan and other documents say
The local plan policies and designations that cover the site's location, read in full, and any site refused elsewhere in the same area for a reason that would apply here. Then every mention of the site, its road or its parish in the council's and parish's other documents, with the words quoted, and the site's entries in older assessment rounds.

## 8. Market and local intelligence
Listings, auctions, sales, promoters, opposition, news.

## 9. Constraints
The constraints that matter, each with what it means and whether it can be designed around.

## 10. Risks you may not have thought of
Each risk in a sentence or two, and what it would take to deal with it.

## 11. Conclusion
How the findings add up: the strongest case for the site, the strongest case against, and how a planning officer would most likely weigh them.

## 12. Verdict
<Approach the owner | Approach the agent or promoter | Park until <trigger> | Drop>, with the reasons in three to five lines, and who to approach first.

## 13. Coverage checklist
Each category: checked (finding), not relevant (reason), could not check (reason). Keep it compact.

## 14. What I need from you
Only things only you can do, each in one plain sentence, and how the verdict could change once each is done. (Omit the section if there is nothing.)

**Next step:** <one line offering the next skill, e.g. "Would you like me to prepare the appraisal now?">
```
